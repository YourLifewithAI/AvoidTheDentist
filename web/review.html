The idea

The surest way to avoid the big dentist is to see the small one

You live one life, from the first tooth to the rocking chair, in a dollhouse cutaway. Time runs by itself like an idle game. You set habits, pick a job, play sports and answer life's little prompts. A hidden model, calibrated against CDC data, decides what happens next, and you see it in the person: an open grin or a hand over the mouth, a dream board filling with trips or a counter stacking up with bills.

Three findings shaped the design. Avoidance can look cheaper on paper: always picking the cheapest fix saves money but costs about three teeth, so the ledger has to count teeth, pain and hidden smiles, not just dollars. Sugar frequency is the biggest habit lever. Birth and microbiome are a small head start, real but dwarfed by habits, which is the no-guilt message we want.

New mechanic · the Acid Clock

A day of eating, the way your teeth feel it

Every snack or sugary drink drops the pH of the plaque on your teeth for a while. Below 5.5, enamel starts to dissolve; exposed roots start sooner, around 6.2. Saliva brings the pH back up, and once it is back above about 6.0 the enamel starts regaining minerals: the day is a tug-of-war between acid and repair. How low it goes and how long it stays down depends on what you eat, when you eat it, and your mouth. This is the Stephan curve, and in the game it is the Acid Clock: the model runs it for every day of a life and adds up the acid time.

New · the backyard garden

Your mouth's ecosystem, without showing a tooth

Most tooth decay and gum disease comes from bacteria that habits feed. In the game that ecosystem is the garden behind the house. Flowers are the health-associated bacteria. Sugar weeds are the acid-loving decay bacteria, and they spread with frequent sugar and long acid time. Gum weeds are the anaerobes that thrive in inflamed gums, tartar and deep pockets. Low saliva shows as dry, cracked soil. A microbiome test is a soil test, and the hygienist is the gardener.

New · the tool shed

Every tool with its evidence, generics first

Products, habits and treatments the game can point to. Each carries an evidence badge and a source. Newer options sit beside the proven ones, clearly labeled, and none of them works alone. Dentist-only items point to your dentist. “Try it” shows the change in the garden above, with the same luck.

The backyard garden

Four lives, four gardens. Prevention Pro's is mostly flowers. The Soda Sipper's is overrun with sugar weeds. The Avoider's gum weeds creep in when nobody pulls them. On a drying medication, the soil cracks.

How much toothpaste for little kids

A parent's choice with a real tradeoff, straight from the sim. The ADA's rice grain under 3 and pea from 3 to 6 keep the cavity protection. A full ribbon adds fluorosis, mostly faint white flecks. Skipping fluoride paste cuts fluorosis but sends early cavities from 28% to 45%.

The tool shed

Shelves for brush and paste, food and drink, the dentist's chair, sleep and body, and tests. Each tool wears its evidence badge; a white tag means ask your dentist.

The main screen

Feelings instead of stats in the status panel, a wallet with speed controls, an event card (“It's been 18 months!”) and the life timeline. The postcards on the fridge are the reminders Maya keeps not answering.

One life, eight moments

Age shows through the sprite: a baby with a rattle, a kid with a dentist's balloon, a teen in a hoodie, a pastry chef at 38, reading glasses at 55, a cane at 75. Pencil marks track her height on the growth chart.

Feelings, not teeth

Smiling freely, hiding a smile, a toothache, a cold sting, grinding at night, dodging the call, a swollen jaw, a clean checkup. This is how the hidden model's states reach the player.

Work and play

A baker tasting all day, a loader clenching under a box, office coffee and stress, energy drinks on the night shift, hockey with and without a guard, soda on the road.

The dentist as a warm place

A fish tank, cartoons on the ceiling, a sticker chart, a smiling hygienist, June grinning in the chair with a balloon. The game never uses drills, blood or a scolding dentist.

The cast

One paper-doll system with five skin tones, twelve heads, fifteen outfits, fifteen expressions and twelve poses.

Helpers, temptations, dreams

Helpers are the idle-game upgrades: real-world nudges that make good habits automatic. Temptations are the acid clocks. Dreams are what the money could have been, pinned to the dream board in the living room.

Day, dusk, night

The sky and the lamps follow the clock, so the house feels lived in whether you check in at breakfast or at midnight.

The Hearth palette

About 70 colors in ramps: ink and paper, wood, warm, green and teal, cool, rose and wallpaper, sky, and five skin ramps.

How it plays

Four ways to live it

15–20 minutes

Live a Life

Birth to 80 in the house. Parents decide early on; you take over as a teen. Event cards, habits, helpers and a year-end ledger lead to your Life Story.

1–3 minutes

What-If Lab

Every choice on one screen, then jump straight to the outcome. Built for curious players and for dental teams chairside.

2–5 minutes

Twin Lives

Pick a moment, change one choice, and watch both houses side by side with the same luck. The game asks “What changed?” before it shows the ledger.

30 seconds

100 Lives Like Yours

An icon array of 100 pixel people who made your choices: how many kept every tooth, needed a root canal, or lost teeth to gum disease.

Is the model honest?

Calibrated against US surveillance data

3,000 random US-like lives compared with CDC figures. The model runs month by month from birth to 80, with 48 teeth, gums, grinding, trauma, fear and avoidance, insurance and savings. About half of its parameters are verified against published sources; the rest are flagged for review by a dental advisor.

Where the model misses: dental fear sits a little below target now that the Acid Clock produces slightly less adult decay (fewer bad visits). People who keep their teeth keep about two more of them at 65+ than today's elders do. Part of that is a cohort effect, since today's 75-year-olds grew up before fluoride toothpaste, but it needs a dental advisor's review.

Your answers, and what's still open

Decisions

  1. Audience: adults, young adults and parents at home. “Live a Life” is the default mode, and cards are written for grown-ups and parents.
  2. A free public game that clinics can use for education. Embeddable and kiosk-friendly, with no clinic branding required.
  3. Fluoride as part of the balance, not the water debate. Water fluoridation stays a small, neutral birth circumstance. The emphasis moves to the daily ebb and flow of demineralization and remineralization, which the Acid Clock already shows from the acid side. Proposed next: draw the repair side, and add a smear-or-pea-size toothpaste choice for parents with mild fluorosis as a possible outcome.
  4. Clinical advisor: you. First review: the Acid Clock food table and critical pH values, then the parameters flagged [U].
  5. Considering: recommendations with affiliate links. Proposed guardrails: the model never knows about products; every item carries an evidence badge; emerging items such as microbiome tests are framed as “see for yourself” tracking; pay can't buy placement; generic options are shown where they exist; and dentist-only items point to the dentist.
  6. Still open: no numbers during play (my proposal), US-only costs for v1, code-drawn art or a pixel artist, and the name.
